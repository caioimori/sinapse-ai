# Blake Snyder Beat Sheet — Save the Cat Deep Reference

> Version: 1.0.0 | Agent: blake-snyder | Domain: screenplay-structure, commercial-narrative

## Overview

Blake Snyder (1957-2009) wrote "Save the Cat! The Last Book on Screenwriting You'll Ever Need" (2005), which became the most widely used screenwriting manual in Hollywood. The Beat Sheet is a page-specific, 15-beat framework for constructing any commercial narrative.

The title "Save the Cat" refers to a technique: the hero should do something likeable early in the story (like saving a cat) so the audience roots for them. It's a scene, but also a philosophy — earn empathy fast.

---

## The 15 Beats (with Page Numbers for 110-Page Screenplay)

| Beat | Page | Function |
|------|------|---------|
| **Opening Image** | p. 1 | A single image that sets tone and establishes the world BEFORE transformation |
| **Theme Stated** | p. 5 | Someone (not the hero) tells the hero what the story is REALLY about — usually dismissed |
| **Set-Up** | pp. 1-10 | Establish the hero's flawed ordinary world — show what needs to change |
| **Catalyst** | p. 12 | The inciting incident — the event that disrupts the status quo. Cannot be undone. |
| **Debate** | pp. 12-25 | The hero asks "Should I go?" — hesitation, fear, weighing options |
| **Break Into Two** | p. 25 | The hero actively CHOOSES to enter Act 2 — their decision, their agency |
| **B Story** | p. 30 | A new relationship begins — usually the love story or mentor relationship that carries the theme |
| **Fun and Games** | pp. 30-55 | The promise of the premise — the fun part, the reason the movie was made |
| **Midpoint** | p. 55 | False victory OR false defeat — raises the stakes before the real trouble starts |
| **Bad Guys Close In** | pp. 55-75 | External pressure mounts; internal doubts deepen; the team falls apart |
| **All Is Lost** | p. 75 | The worst moment — opposite of the midpoint's tone (if midpoint was victory, All Is Lost is defeat) |
| **Dark Night of the Soul** | pp. 75-85 | The hero sits with loss — reflection before the breakthrough |
| **Break Into Three** | p. 85 | The solution: A + B story cross — the insight from the B Story solves the A Story problem |
| **Finale** | pp. 85-110 | The hero executes the new plan — not the old approach |
| **Final Image** | p. 110 | Mirror of Opening Image — shows how completely the world has changed |

## The Double Pump Structure

Act 1 (pages 1-25): Set-Up
Act 2A (pages 25-55): Fun and Games — things go well or better than expected
**Midpoint** (page 55): The spike — everything shifts
Act 2B (pages 55-85): Bad Guys Close In — things deteriorate
Act 3 (pages 85-110): Finale — resolution through transformed hero

The midpoint is the structural key. It divides Act 2 into two very different halves. Without a strong midpoint, Act 2 becomes a monotonous middle.

## Save the Cat Moment — The Likeability Principle

Within the first 10 pages, show the hero doing something that earns audience sympathy. This is not just about saving a literal cat — it's about demonstrating:

- **Competence** — hero is good at something
- **Humor** — hero can laugh at themselves
- **Vulnerability** — hero has a human weakness
- **Generosity** — hero cares about others
- **Injustice** — hero is treated unfairly (we root against the unfair situation)

Without this, audiences don't invest. The technique works because it creates **emotional identification before the story demands anything from the viewer.**

## The 10 Genre Types

Snyder argued there are only 10 fundamental story genres (not by subject matter, but by structure and emotional contract):

| Genre | Logline Pattern | Examples |
|-------|----------------|---------|
| **Monster in the House** | A monster terrorizes a confined group | Jaws, Alien, Halloween, The Silence of the Lambs |
| **Golden Fleece** | Hero goes on a road trip seeking a prize | The Wizard of Oz, Star Wars, Raiders of the Lost Ark |
| **Out of the Bottle** | A wish is granted; chaos ensues | Liar Liar, Bruce Almighty, Freaky Friday, The Mask |
| **Dude with a Problem** | An ordinary person faces extraordinary circumstances | Die Hard, The Fugitive, Gravity, Taken |
| **Rites of Passage** | A painful life event forces growth | Ordinary People, Kramer vs Kramer, About Schmidt |
| **Buddy Love** | Two incompatible characters must work together | Planes Trains and Automobiles, Lethal Weapon, My Big Fat Greek Wedding |
| **Whydunit** | A detective uncovers hidden truths | Chinatown, Memento, Gone Girl, The Sixth Sense |
| **Fool Triumphant** | An underdog succeeds against all expectations | Forrest Gump, Being There, Legally Blonde |
| **Institutionalized** | Character must choose between group and self | One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, MASH, Animal House |
| **Superhero** | A unique person must accept their power and responsibility | Spider-Man, Batman Begins, The Matrix |

**Why genres matter:** Each genre creates a specific emotional promise. Audiences know what they're signing up for. Breaking genre convention without purpose creates unsatisfied expectations.

## Applying Beat Sheet to Business Presentations

The beat sheet adapts powerfully to presentations and pitches:

| Beat Sheet Beat | Presentation Equivalent |
|-----------------|------------------------|
| Opening Image | Opening hook — the vivid "before" scenario |
| Theme Stated | The central thesis stated by someone else (quote, stat, customer voice) |
| Catalyst | The market shift, the problem that can't be ignored |
| Break Into Two | "So we built something different" — the pivot point |
| Fun and Games | The demo, the case studies, the compelling evidence |
| Midpoint | The moment of highest engagement — the S.T.A.R. moment |
| All Is Lost | "Without this, here's what happens" — the consequence slide |
| Break Into Three | The solution synthesis — how the pieces fit |
| Finale | The vision, the ask, the call to action |
| Final Image | The "after" picture — contrast with Opening Image |

## Snyder's Logline Formula

A logline is a one-sentence story summary that contains all the essential dramatic elements:

```
When [CATALYST happens to] [a FLAWED HERO with a primal goal],
[they must] [achieve a GOAL] 
before [the STAKES/CONSEQUENCE plays out].
```

Examples:
- "A shark terrorizes a beach resort, and an unlikely trio of men must kill it before it destroys the town." (Jaws)
- "A widower's son is taken by a diver, forcing the overprotective father on a dangerous ocean journey." (Finding Nemo)

A strong logline contains: irony, compelling mental picture, audience sense, cost estimate, and title hint.

## The All Is Lost "Whiff of Death"

Snyder observed that the All Is Lost moment almost always involves a literal or symbolic "whiff of death." This can be:

- **Physical death** — Character is in mortal danger
- **Professional death** — Career is destroyed
- **Relational death** — The most important relationship ends
- **Spiritual death** — The hero loses their sense of who they are

The whiff of death works because it triggers the audience's existential empathy. When the hero faces death (in any form), the viewer asks the universal question: "What would I do?"

## Beat Sheet for Short-Form Content

Compressing the beat sheet for blog posts, social content, and short videos:

| Beat (Compressed) | Blog Post | Short Video |
|-------------------|-----------|------------|
| Opening Image | Headline + opener | First 3 seconds |
| Catalyst | The problem stated | "Here's the thing nobody tells you" |
| Fun and Games | The core argument | Steps, reveals, evidence |
| All Is Lost | "Most people get this wrong" | The mistake or consequence |
| Break Into Three | "Here's what actually works" | The insight |
| Final Image | Call to action + contrast | Payoff line + CTA |

## Cross-Reference

- **Dan Harmon's Story Circle** compresses similar beats into 8 steps without page constraints
- **Nancy Duarte's Sparkline** mirrors the Midpoint/All Is Lost tension with "What Is / What Could Be" oscillation
